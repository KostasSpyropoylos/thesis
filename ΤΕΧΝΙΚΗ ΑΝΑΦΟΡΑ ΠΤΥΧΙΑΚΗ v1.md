## 1. Abstract

This document delves into the implementation and evaluation of
alternative techniques for the widely used **K-Means clustering
algorithm**. K-Means stands as one of the most popular unsupervised
machine learning algorithms for data analysis, particularly in
applications where similar objects need to be grouped into distinct
clusters.

However, before proceeding to the technical implementation, it is
essential to precisely define the core problem we aim to solve. The
objective can be summarized in three main pillars:

1.  **Defining Spherical Clusters:** The construction of circular or
    spherical clusters that are strictly defined by a center point and a
    specific radius.

2.  **Minimizing Overlap:** Ensuring that these clusters have the
    minimum possible overlap between them to maintain distinct
    boundaries.

3.  **Complete Coverage:** Ensuring the clusters are positioned such
    that they encompass all data points within the set.

### Challenges in Traditional K-Means

The efficiency of K-Means, especially in large-volume and
high-dimensional datasets, can be hindered by computational complexity,
particularly during the search for each data point\'s nearest centroid.
Significant challenges arise in datasets characterized by **overlapping
clusters** and **skewed distributions**. In such scenarios, the
traditional K-Means approach can lead to suboptimal results, with points
being assigned to incorrect clusters due to the algorithm\'s sensitivity
to initial centroid selections and the mandatory assignment of each
point to a single cluster. This can result in lower quality clusters,
exhibiting increased intra-cluster variance and reduced inter-cluster
separation.

### Proposed Solution and Spatial Indexing

Within the scope of this work, we focus on examining and practically
applying two spatial indexing structures: the **Grid** and the
**R-Tree**.

These structures are adopted with the aim of accelerating the
point-to-cluster assignment phase, thereby reducing the overall
computational time of K-Means and improving its scalability. Beyond just
speed, the proposed methods provide a framework for minimizing cluster
overlaps and enhancing cluster quality, even in asymmetrically
distributed data. Through a systematic analysis, we will evaluate the
impact of these techniques on the algorithm\'s performance, considering
both execution speed and clustering quality on a dataset with overlaps
and asymmetry.

## 2. Explanation of Challenges

To fully grasp the necessity of the proposed optimizations, it\'s
important to analyze the challenges that **overlapping** and **skewed
data** pose for the K-Means algorithm:

- **Overlapping Datasets:**

  - **Problem:** In datasets where clusters are not clearly separated
    and there are regions where points from different clusters are in
    close proximity, K-Means struggles to accurately delineate cluster
    boundaries. This often leads to **misassignments** of points to
    clusters, reducing the **cohesion** within a cluster and increasing
    the overlap between clusters.

  - **Impact on Quality:** Cluster quality degrades as clusters are
    neither compact nor distinct. Metrics like the **Silhouette Score**
    will show lower values, indicating poor separation between clusters.

- **Skewed Datasets:**

  - **Problem:** When the distribution of data points is not uniform
    (e.g., there are regions of very high density and others of very low
    density), K-Means can be \"pulled\" towards dense areas, ignoring or
    incorrectly clustering sparser points. Computational complexity is
    also affected, as searching in sparse parts of the space can be
    inefficient.

  - **Impact on Quality:** Clustering quality can be heterogeneous, with
    some clusters being very compact and others scattered, reflecting
    the data\'s asymmetry rather than the inherent cluster structure.

## 3. Evaluation Criteria and Goals of Optimization

The integration of Grid and R-Tree structures is driven by specific
objectives aimed at improving clustering performance, particularly when
dealing with challenging data distributions. The primary criteria
guiding the selection and evaluation of these methods are:

### **3.1.** **Minimizing Overlaps Between Clusters:** 

A key goal is to enhance the distinctiveness of the resulting clusters.
The spatial indexing capabilities of Grid and R-Tree are hypothesized to
facilitate more precise nearest neighbor assignments, thereby reducing
the misclassification of data points that lie in ambiguous or
overlapping regions. This is expected to lead to clusters with clearer
boundaries and less inter-cluster mixing.

### **3.2. Providing Higher Quality Clusters:** 

Beyond just reducing overlaps, the objective is to achieve overall
superior cluster quality. This encompasses both high intra-cluster
cohesion (points within a cluster are close to each other) and strong
inter-cluster separation (clusters are well-separated from each other).
By accelerating and refining the **centroid assignment process**, these
methods are expected to enable algorithms to converge to more **optimal
cluster configurations**, even in the presence of **noise** or **complex
data geometries**.

### **3.3** **Ensuring Inclusion of All Data Points:** 

> A fundamental characteristic of K-Means is its partitioning nature,
> where every data

point is assigned to exactly one cluster. This stands in contrast to
certain density-based clustering algorithms, such as **DBSCAN
(Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)**, which
are designed to identify and exclude \"noise\" or \"outlier\" points
that do not meet specific density thresholds. While DBSCAN\'s ability to
identify outliers is valuable in some contexts, for applications
requiring a complete partitioning of the dataset, K-Means is preferred.
The Grid and R-Tree structures, due to their spatial division nature,
are expected to be more efficient in clustering. Therefore, a critical
criterion for the proposed methods is to maintain the K-Means\' inherent
ability to **assign every data point to a cluster, even those that might
be considered outliers by density-based approaches**, ensuring a
comprehensive grouping of the entire dataset.

These criteria will serve as the benchmarks against which the
performance of the Grid-based and R-Tree algorithms will be rigorously
evaluated against the standard K-Means implementation.

### 4.1. Grid

#### What it is: 

> A Grid, or mesh, is a simple and intuitive spatial indexing structure.
> Essentially, the data space is divided into a grid of equally sized
> cells. Each cell can store references to the data points contained
> within its boundaries.

###### Grid Usage Example

![](media/image1.png){width="6.5in" height="1.8194444444444444in"}

###### Grid Initialization and Data Mapping Algorithm

This algorithm outlines the process of partitioning a 2D spatial domain
into a grid structure based on the extents of a dataset.

###### 1. Spatial Domain Determination

First, the algorithm calculates the bounding box of the dataset to
define the limits of the Grid:

- xmin, ymin: The minimum coordinates across all data points.

- xmax, ymax: The maximum coordinates across all data points.

###### 2. Grid Construction

The Grid is initialized as an empty structure with MxM dimensions:

- Dimensions: Define a resolution M (number of cells per axis).

- Cell Width $\Delta x$: Calculated as $(xmax\  - xmin)\  \div \ m$

- Cell Height $\Delta y$: Calculated as $(ymax\  - ymin)\  \div \ m$

The zip() function in Python is used to combine two or more iterables
(like lists, tuples, strings, dictionaries, etc.) into a single iterator
of tuples. Each tuple contains elements that share the same index across
the input iterables.

###### The fit Process: Data Assignment and Geometric Calculation

The fit method acts as the engine that populates the Grid and calculates
the physical boundaries of the resulting clusters.

###### 1. Spatial Look-up (find_cell)

To ensure every point is mapped correctly without exceeding the grid
boundaries, the algorithm uses a \"clamping\" logic:

- **Coordinate Normalization**: It calculates the index by subtracting
  the minimum boundary (xmin) and dividing by the cell step
  ($\Delta x$).

- **Boundary Protection:** The min(max(\...)) logic ensures that even
  points exactly on the edge or slightly outside due to floating-point
  errors are safely tucked into the first (0) or last (m-1) cell.

###### 2. The Iterative Assignment

Using the zip()-style data structure discussed previously, the algorithm
iterates through every point:

1.  **Object Creation:** Converts raw coordinates (x, y) into a Geometry
    object.

2.  **Cell Retrieval:** Calls findCell to identify the specific bucket
    in the $m\ x\ m$ matrix.

3.  **Point Insertion:** Adds the point to that cell\'s internal list.

###### 3. Finalizing Cluster Geometry (get_radius)

Once all points are assigned, the algorithm triggers a final calculation
phase:

- **Centroid Update:** Calculates the mean center of all points within
  each cell.

- **Radius Calculation:** Determines the distance from the centroid to
  the furthest point in that cell. This fulfills the goal of creating
  spherical clusters defined by a center and radius.

![](media/image5.png){width="6.5in" height="6.541666666666667in"}

#### Contribution to reducing overlaps and improving quality:

- **Restricted Search:** During the assignment phase, the search for the
  nearest centroid is limited to centroids that are in neighboring
  cells. This **reduces the likelihood of misassignments** to distant
  centroids that could lead to increased overlap, as the algorithm
  focuses on locally closest centroids within reasonable spatial
  proximity.

- **Identification of \"Boundary\" Points:** Points located at the
  boundaries between cells containing different clusters can be analyzed
  more carefully, potentially allowing for the application of more
  sophisticated criteria for their assignment, thereby **minimizing
  overlaps in these boundary zones**.

- **Inclusion of All Data Points:** Crucially, the Grid structure, when
  integrated does **not exclude any data points from the clustering
  process**. This aligns directly with our criterion to include all data
  points, ensuring that every observation is considered in the final
  partitioning, making it suitable for applications where every data
  point must belong to a group.

### 4.2. R-Tree

#### What it is:

> An R-Tree (Rectangular Tree) is a hierarchical, tree-based data
> structure specifically designed for efficient indexing of
> multi-dimensional data, especially geometric objects like points,
> lines, and rectangles. Each node in the R-Tree corresponds to a
> **Minimum Bounding Rectangle (MBR)** that encloses all the objects or
> sub-MBRs of its children. The leaf nodes of the tree contain the data
> points themselves.

###### R-Tree Spatial Indexing: Loading and Construction

The load_data_from_csv method serves as the entry point for building the
R-Tree. Unlike the Grid, which pre-calculates boundaries, the R-Tree is
built incrementally.

###### 1. Efficient Data Streaming (Chunking)

To handle large-volume datasets without exhausting memory, the algorithm
uses chunked processing:

- pd.read_csv(\..., chunksize=1000): Instead of loading the entire file,
  the algorithm processes 1,000 rows at a time.

- Vectorized Extraction: It converts columns to NumPy arrays
  (to_numpy()) for high-speed access before iterating.

###### 2. The Insertion Logic

For every point in the dataset, the R-Tree creates a spatial
representation:

- Point-to-Rectangle Mapping: Since R-Trees typically index \"Bounding
  Boxes,\" a point is represented as a zero-area rectangle where
  xmin=xmax and ymin=ymax.

- rtree_index.insert: The index dynamically organizes these points into
  a tree structure. As points are added, the tree creates internal nodes
  (bounding boxes) that group nearby points together.

#### Contribution to reducing overlaps and improving quality:

- **Dynamic Adaptation to Data Distribution:** Unlike the static Grid,
  the R-Tree **dynamically adapts to the actual data distribution**,
  creating MBRs that compactly enclose clusters. This is particularly
  beneficial in **skewed data** and data with **natural overlaps**, as
  the tree structures better reflect the natural \"clumps\" of data.

- **More Accurate Nearest Neighbor Search:** The R-Tree\'s ability to
  perform efficient nearest neighbor searches means that for each data
  point, we can more accurately locate the truly closest centroids. This
  **drastically reduces the probability of assigning a point to an
  incorrect centroid** that is far away, even if an intermediate
  centroid appears to be the closest in an unoptimized search.

- **Minimization of MBR Overlaps:** R-Tree construction and optimization
  algorithms often aim to **reduce overlap between MBRs** at each level
  of the tree. This is reflected in the clustering of the data: if MBRs
  do not significantly overlap, it suggests that the underlying clusters
  are also well-separated. By applying this characteristic to centroid
  searching, the creation of more distinct and less overlapping clusters
  is encouraged.

- **Improved Management of \"Boundary\" Regions:** The R-Tree can more
  effectively identify the areas where different clusters \"meet.\" This
  allows the algorithm to more intelligently handle points that lie in
  these boundary zones, leading to more accurate assignments and,
  consequently, **higher quality, less overlapping clusters**.

- **Inclusion of All Data Points:** Similar to the Grid approach, the
  integration of an R-Tree ensures that **all data points are considered
  for assignment to a cluster**. The R-Tree primarily serves as an
  efficient lookup structure for spatial queries, not as a mechanism for
  determining core points or outliers based on density. Therefore, every
  point within the dataset will be assigned to its closest centroid,
  aligning with our objective to ensure no data is left unclustered.

- **Negative Skew (**skew \< 0**):** Biases coordinates toward the
  maximum defined boundary (max).

> This process enables the simulation of data concentration in specific
> geographical regions, providing a targeted stress test for the spatial
> algorithms.

> **Purpose:** The Grid class implements the core logic for creating a
> grid, assigning points to cells, calculating centroids and radii for
> each \"cluster\" within the cells, and computing the overlap between
> these clusters.

###### Algorithm Description:

> 1\. Initialization & Mapping
>
> The algorithm divides the spatial domain (xmin to xmax and ymin to
> ymax) into an $m\ x\ m$ matrix.

- Direct Indexing: Instead of searching, the *findCell* method maps any
  (x,y) point directly to its cell index:
  $i\  = \left\lbrack (x - \ xmin)\ /\ \Delta x) \right\rbrack$.

- Clamping: Boundary checks ensure points on the far edges are safely
  assigned to the perimeter cells.

> 2\. Geometric Fitting (fit & get_clusters)
>
> Once data is loaded into the cells, the algorithm transitions from
> square boundaries to circular clusters. By executing get_clusters
> after the fit method, the system returns a list of clusters (e.g.,
> {$C_{1}(K_{1},R_{1})$, $C_{2}(K_{2},R_{2})$, ...} where K=(x,y), R

- Centroids: The center of each cluster is calculated as the mean
  position of all points within that specific cell.

- Radius: The radius is defined as the distance from the centroid to the
  furthest point in the cell. This guarantees 100% coverage of the data.

3\. Overlap Quantification

To evaluate clustering quality, the class calculates the \"collision\"
between circles:

- Detection: Two clusters overlap if the Euclidean distance between
  their centroids is equal or less than the sum of their radii
  ($d\  \leq \ r1\  + \ r2\$).

- Area Calculation: The compute_grid_overlap method uses circular
  trigonometry to calculate the precise area of intersection. It handles
  three cases: No overlap, one cluster contained within another, or
  partial (lens-shaped) intersection.

    points.

2.  **Overlap Complexity:** O(M\^2) (where M is total cells) Checks each

    raw data points, calculated centroids, and the resulting circular

##### 6.2.2 RTreeSpatialAnalyzer Class

**Purpose:** The RTreeSpatialAnalyzer class is designed for the
efficient management of spatial data, utilizing the R-tree data
structure for indexing, by making use of the rtree library**.**

###### Algorithm Description:

1\. Initialization & Structure

The algorithm organizes spatial data into a height-balanced tree
structure rather than a fixed grid.

- **Hierarchical Grouping:** Objects are grouped into Minimum Bounding
  Rectangles (MBRs). Leaf nodes contain the actual spatial objects (or
  pointers to them), while internal nodes contain pointers to child
  nodes and the MBR that encompasses all data in that child.

- **Dynamic Bounds:** Unlike the fixed ${xmin/xmax}_{}$of a grid, the
  boundaries in an R-Tree are dynamic. The root node expands to cover
  the entire spatial extent of the data, adapting as new points are
  added.

2\. Geometric Fitting (Insertion & Adjustment)

Once data is inserted, the algorithm fits the tree structure to the data
distribution by minimizing dead space.

- **Least Enlargement:** To insert a new object, the algorithm uses the
  ***ChooseLeaf*** method. It traverses the tree to find the node that
  would require the least increase in area to accommodate the new
  object, keeping rectangles tight.

- **Node Splitting:** When a node exceeds its maximum capacity
  (branching factor), it splits into two. Heuristics are applied to
  distribute entries between the two new nodes in a way that minimizes
  total area and overlap.

- **Upward Propagation:** After an insertion or split, the algorithm
  propagates changes up the tree, adjusting the MBRs of parent nodes to
  ensure they strictly enclose their updated children.

3\. Overlap Qualification (Leaf Clusters)

To evaluate the quality of the geometric fitting, the algorithm computes
the Total Overlap Area between the generated leaf clusters (e.g.,
{$C_{1}(K_{1},R_{1})$, $C_{2}(K_{2},R_{2})$, ...} where K=(x,y), R
radius, C centroid).

The compute_leaves_overlap method iterates through all unique pairs of
clusters to calculate the intersection area based on the distance (d)
between their centroids and their respective radii ($r_{1}$,$r_{2}$).
The class calculates the \"collision\" between circles:

- Detection: Two clusters overlap if the Euclidean distance between
  their centroids is equal or less than the sum of their radii
  ($d\  \leq \ r1\  + \ r2\$).

- Area Calculation: The compute_leaves_overlap method uses circular
  trigonometry to calculate the precise area of intersection. It handles
  three cases: No overlap, one cluster contained within another, or
  partial (lens-shaped) intersection.

4\. Summary of Key Metrics

- **Complexity:** Average search complexity is $O(\log_{M}N)$ (where M
  is the node capacity), vastly outperforming linear searches on large
  datasets. Worst-case can approach O(N) if overlap is high.

- **Space Efficiency:** Space complexity is O(N). The tree remains
  balanced, ensuring the height grows logarithmically with the number of
  items.

- **Visual Output:** Provides a view of nested rectangles, where large
  \"parent\" boxes contain smaller \"child\" boxes, visualizing the
  spatial density and clustering of the data.

#### 7.1.1 Overlap Metric:

Mathematics of Overlapping Circles

Calculating the overlap area between two circles is a classic geometry
problem that requires handling three distinct spatial relationships
based on the distance (d) between their centers and their radii ($r_{1}$
and $r_{2}$).

1\. The Variables

- $C_{1}$**,** $C_{2}$: The centers of the two circles.

- $r_{1}$**,** $r_{2}$: The radii of the two circles.

- $d_{}$: The Euclidean distance between the centers, calculated as
  $d_{}$ = \| $C_{1}$ - $C_{2}$ \|.

Case 1: No Overlap

If the distance between centers is greater than or equal to the sum of
the radii, the circles do not touch or intersect.

- Condition: $d\  \geq \ \ \ |\ r_{1}\  + \ \ r_{2}\ |\$

- Overlap Area = 0

Case 2: Complete Enclosure

If the distance is small enough that one circle fits entirely inside the
other, the overlap area is simply the area of the smaller circle.

- Condition: $d\  \leq \ \ \ |\ r_{1}\  - \ \ r_{2}\ |\$

- Overlap Area = $\pi\ x\ min(r_{1},r_{2})^{2}$

Case 3: Partial Intersection

This is the most complex case, where the circles intersect at two
points, forming a lens-shaped overlap.

- Condition:
  $\ |\ r_{1}\  - \ \ r_{2}\ |\  < \ d\  < r_{1}\  + \ \ r_{2}\$

The Formula:

The area is calculated by summing the areas of the two circular sectors
formed by the intersection points and subtracting the area of the two
triangles formed by the chord connecting the intersection points.

$Overlap\ Area\  = \ {r_{1}}^{2}cos^{- 1}(\frac{d^{2} + {r_{1}}^{2} - {r_{2}}^{2}}{2dr_{1}})\  + \ {r_{2}}^{2}cos^{- 1}(\frac{d^{2}\  + \ {r_{2}}^{2} - {r_{1}}^{2}}{2dr_{2}})\  - \frac{1}{2}\ \ \$

#### 7.1.3 Normal Distribution Dataset:

##### Dataset description and visualization

The function produces a synthetic dataset of spatial points distributed
according to a Gaussian (Normal) curve, simulating real-world scenarios
where data is concentrated around a central location (the mean) and
dissipates outwards based on a specified standard deviation. To ensure
the integrity of the spatial normalization, the function applies an
outlier protection step that clips the extreme top and bottom
percentiles (defaulting to 0.1%), preventing rare anomalies from skewing
the scaling process. The data is then Min-Max normalized and linearly
scaled to fit within defined geographical boundaries (e.g., -4.0 to
4.0), resulting in a finalized dataset that retains the bell-curve
density properties of the normal distribution while strictly adhering to
the specified coordinate limits.
