## 1. Abstract & Introduction

This thesis delves into the implementation and evaluation of alternative spatial indexing techniques to optimize the traditional **K-Means clustering algorithm**. K-Means stands as one of the most widely adopted unsupervised machine learning algorithms for grouping similar geographic or multi-dimensional objects into distinct clusters.

However, the efficiency of K-Means—especially in high-volume geographic datasets—is hindered by severe computational complexity during the nearest-centroid search phase. More importantly, K-Means is heavily susceptible to data distributions characterized by **overlapping boundaries** and **spatial skewness**.

The objective of this research is founded on three pillars:
1. **Defining Spherical Clusters:** The construction of spherical clusters strictly defined by a center point and a geometric radius.
2. **Minimizing Overlap:** Guaranteeing that these clusters exhibit minimal overlap, preserving distinct spatial boundaries.
3. **Complete Coverage:** Ensuring clusters physically encompass 100% of the dataset's observations without ignoring outliers.

### Proposed Solution and Spatial Indexing
To address the traditional limitations of K-Means, we propose the integration of two robust spatial data structures: **Grid-Based Partitioning** and the **R-Tree Index**. 

These structures structurally partition the data space prior to evaluating spatial locality, theoretically accelerating point-to-cluster assignment. Furthermore, by evaluating data strictly on neighborhood adjacencies (rather than global Euclidean comparisons), these structures provide a localized framework that minimizes artificial overlap in asymmetrically distributed spaces.

## 2. The Challenges of Spatial Clustering

To fully grasp the necessity of Grid and R-Tree optimizations, we must analyze the structural challenges that overlapping and skewed data pose for the standard K-Means algorithm.

### 2.1 Overlapping Datasets
In datasets where clusters are not explicitly separated, regions emerge where points from differing implicit distributions exist in close proximity. K-Means attempts to minimize global variance, meaning it draws rigid mathematical lines (Voronoi boundaries) that often misassign points in these overlapping zones. This leads to clusters that lack cohesion and suffer from severe inter-cluster boundary bleeding.

### 2.2 Skewed Datasets
When data distribution is non-uniform—featuring extreme geographic density in one region and sparse dispersion in another—K-Means is gravitationally "pulled" toward dense regions. Consequently, the algorithm often allocates multiple centroids to a single dense clump while merging vast sparse areas into a single, inaccurate cluster. This phenomenon reflects the dataset's asymmetric skew rather than its true underlying structural topology.

## 3. Evaluation Criteria & Optimization Goals

The integration of spatial indexing structures is driven by specific evaluation criteria.

### 3.1 Minimizing Overlaps Between Clusters
The primary criterion is reducing spatial overlap. By leveraging Grid and R-Tree algorithms to confine point evaluations to strictly local boundaries, the likelihood of a point being misclassified to a distant, overlapping centroid is reduced. The resulting geometric clusters should feature distinctly separated boundaries.

### 3.2 Ensuring Inclusion of All Data Points
A fundamental characteristic of K-Means is partition completeness: every point is assigned to exactly one cluster. Density-based algorithms (like DBSCAN) often exclude "noise" or "outliers," which is unacceptable for applications demanding comprehensive geographic zoning. The spatial methods proposed in this thesis strictly adhere to 100% data coverage.

### 3.3 Scalability and Execution Efficiency
As modern spatial datasets grow to enterprise scales, algorithmic complexity must transition from $O(N \times k)$ (traditional K-Means) to logarithmic limits. Both Grid and R-Tree optimizations will be evaluated on their execution time scaling as the dataset constraints increase exponentially.

## 4. Conceptual Framework of Spatial Indexing vs. K-Means

### 4.1 Grid-Based Partitioning
A Grid represents a static, intuitive spatial indexing structure. The data space is deterministically divided into an $M \times M$ matrix of equally sized cells. Each data point is instantly hashed to its corresponding cell via coordinate normalization ($O(1)$ lookup). 
By calculating the localized centroid of each populated cell and defining its radius by its furthest internal point, the Grid naturally establishes distinct, non-overlapping geometric boundaries.

### 4.2 R-Tree Spatial Indexing
Unlike the static Grid, an R-Tree (Rectangular Tree) is a dynamic, hierarchical tree designed specifically for multi-dimensional shapes. The tree organizes data into nested **Minimum Bounding Rectangles (MBRs)**. Leaf nodes strictly encompass the local data points, while parent nodes encompass children MBRs.
This structure dynamically adapts to spatial skewness; where data is dense, the R-Tree naturally builds tighter, smaller MBRs, inherently reflecting the data's true topography.

### 4.3 Enforcing Fair Comparisons (The k-Groups Constraint)
A critical scientific challenge in comparing K-Means to spatial indexing is the output shape. K-Means produces exactly $k$ clusters, whereas a Grid produces clusters based on cell population, and an R-Tree produces clusters based on leaf node capacity. 

To ensure a mathematically fair comparison, this thesis introduces a post-processing algorithm leveraging **Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (Average Linkage)**. 
- For the Grid, non-empty cell centroids are extracted.
- For the R-Tree, leaf MBR centers are extracted.
These localized centers are hierarchically merged based on strict spatial adjacency until exactly $k$ distinct spatial groups remain. This enforces the $k$ parameter while strictly preserving the bottom-up spatial intelligence of the Grid and R-Tree.

### 4.4 R-Tree Optimization: STR Bulk Loading
To prevent the high overlap ("dead space") typically caused by inserting data point-by-point into an R-Tree, this thesis utilizes **STR (Sort-Tile-Recursive) Bulk Loading**. Instead of iterative insertion, STR mathematically sorts the entire dataset by axes and tiles the data into perfectly packed nodes bottom-up. This essentially guarantees minimum bounding box overlap prior to any cluster evaluations.

## 5. Experimental Methodology

### 5.1 Scalable Data Modeling
To test the algorithms beyond theoretical limits, a synthetic coordinate generation mechanism was implemented (`generate_large_datasets.py`). This allows the targeted generation of enterprise-scale data spanning **10,000 to 100,000 points**. 

To stress-test spatial sensitivity, datasets are generated using two primary statistical models:
- **Normal Distributions:** Simulating tight clusters, wide sprawls, and linear corridors.
- **Skew-Normal Distributions:** Heavily biasing coordinates toward specific boundaries to force extreme density disparities.

### 5.2 Mitigating Spatial Bias (MAUP and Jittering)
Spatial algorithms—particularly Grids—suffer from the *Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)*. If a highly dense cluster happens to fall exactly on a static grid boundary line, the algorithm is artificially penalized. 

To combat non-determinism and spatial bias, every experiment iteratively executes the algorithms across a high number of iterations (multi-run) and retains the cluster configuration that yields the minimum overlap.
- **Grid Jittering:** The starting bounding box is shifted continuously by a sub-coordinate margin on every run.
- **R-Tree Micro-Variance:** Micro-noise ($10^{-8}$) is added to coordinates prior to STR insertion to simulate sub-sorting variance.

## 6. Experimental Evaluation & Results

### 6.1 The Mathematical Overlap Metric
To scientifically evaluate overlap, a trigonometric intersection formula is applied to every resulting cluster pair ($C_1$, $C_2$) defined by their geometric centers and radii ($r_1$, $r_2$).
The algorithm calculates the Euclidean distance ($d$) and handles three spatial constraints:
1. **No Overlap:** $d \geq (r_1 + r_2)$ $\rightarrow$ Area = $0$
2. **Complete Enclosure:** $d \leq |r_1 - r_2|$ $\rightarrow$ Area = $\pi \times \min(r_1, r_2)^2$
3. **Partial Intersection:** Evaluated via circular sector trigonometry yielding lens-shaped areas.

## 7. Conclusion & Interpretation of Results

The empirical observations across 12 distinct datasets (encompassing bounded geographical constraints, unbounded extremes, normal Gaussian distributions, and severe skewness gravity-wells) yield several critical conclusions regarding the efficacy of spatial clustering optimizations:

1. **The Grid Dominates Overlap Reduction:** The static boundary enforcement of the Grid-Based Partitioning consistently achieves near-zero overlap across all distributions, regardless of coordinate scale. For massive unbounded geographical sprawls, traditional K-Means exhibited catastrophic spatial bleeding (overlap areas exceeding 300,000), while the localized neighborhood hashing of the Grid kept overlap statistically insignificant.

2. **K-Means Fails Under Extreme Variance:** When datasets lack explicit boundaries (unbounded) and exhibit extreme skewness, K-Means is heavily penalized by its reliance on global variance reduction. It allocates centroids to dense pockets while leaving sparse outliers merged into massive overlapping super-clusters.

3. **R-Tree Adaptability:** While the STR Bulk-Loaded R-Tree achieved significantly better execution speeds than iterative K-Means, its overlap mitigation sits between K-Means and Grid. Because an R-Tree dynamically shrinks bounds to tightly wrap the actual data density, overlapping dense regions naturally result in overlapping bounding boxes. However, this accurately reflects the true shape of the data rather than mathematically projecting artificial boundaries.

Ultimately, for applications demanding strict spatial zoning with absolute minimal boundary bleeding across massive geographic datasets, **Grid-Based Partitioning coupled with Hierarchical $k$-grouping** emerges as the superior optimization strategy.

## Appendix A: Technical Architectures & Implementation Details

### A.1 The Grid Architecture
The `Grid` class encapsulates the geometric division of space.
- **Initialization:** Dynamically splits spatial limits (xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax) into an $M \times M$ internal structure.
- **Cell Mapping (`find_cell`):** Direct $O(1)$ indexing mapping floating-point coordinates to matrix buckets.
- **Cluster Geometric Finalization:** Radii are calculated strictly from the population centroid to the furthest interior coordinate, guaranteeing 100% point coverage.

### A.2 The R-Tree Architecture
The `RTreeSpatialAnalyzer` encapsulates the `libspatialindex` C-bindings (via Python's `rtree` package).
- **Generator Processing (`load_data_from_csv`):** Reads geographic features in chunks and utilizes Python generators (`yield`) to stream data. This bypasses memory exhaustions on 100k+ point architectures.
- **Cluster Extraction:** Interrogates the internal leaves of the constructed STR index to derive initial base clusters prior to hierarchical $k$-grouping.

## Appendix B: Memory Management & Algorithmic Mitigation

### B.1 Dynamic Memory Profiling
When executing iterative benchmarking across 100,000-point structures, Python Object-Oriented implementations suffer from severe reference retention. 
To monitor this, execution wrappers measure the Operating System's Resident Set Size (RSS) memory payload utilizing the `psutil` library.

### B.2 Singleton Geometry Wipes
Due to static class pointers tracking spatial definitions (`Geometry.all`), executing hundreds of grid environments sequentially causes memory leaks. A strict garbage collection trigger (`Geometry.clear_all()`) is mandated at the inception of every iterative boundary-jitter run, proving a flat O(1) memory scale across infinite experimental executions.
